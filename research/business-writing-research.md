# Business/Product Communication — Research Notes

Scope: product requirements, acceptance criteria, business rules, internal engineering/stakeholder communication. Explicitly **not** marketing/advertising copywriting — that literature (AIDA/PAS/FAB, ad compliance) is out of scope for this skill and is not researched here.

## Acceptance criteria

- **[Source-backed principle]** Effective acceptance criteria focus on user-visible behavior, not implementation details — "Given a logged-in user" not "Given the UserService returns a valid JWT". (TestQuality, "How to Write Effective Gherkin Acceptance Criteria"; AltexSoft, "Acceptance Criteria: Purposes, Types, Examples and Best Practices")
- **[Source-backed principle]** Each scenario should be self-contained, atomic, and test one path — split happy path, error states, and edge cases into separate scenarios rather than branching inside one. (TestQuality; Ranorex, "When to Use Given-When-Then Acceptance Criteria")
- **[Source-backed principle]** Vague qualifiers ("fast", "user-friendly", "good UX") make criteria untestable — every criterion needs a verifiable condition. (TestQuality)
- **[Source-backed principle]** Preconditions should be stated explicitly even when they seem obvious, because an implicit precondition becomes ambiguous the moment the codebase changes under it. (TestQuality)
- **[Project decision]** For Thai acceptance criteria specifically: keep Given/When/Then as English structural keywords (they function as a recognized convention/label, not prose) and write the content clause in natural Thai. Do not translate "Given/When/Then" into Thai equivalents (เมื่อ/ถ้า/ดังนั้น) inside a Gherkin-style doc — mixing the label language with the content language is fine here because the labels are structural markers, not sentence text. This mirrors how English technical keywords survive untranslated in otherwise-Thai technical prose generally (see [terminology-research.md](terminology-research.md)).
- **[Open question]** Whether Thai-language BDD tooling in the wild actually localizes these keywords (Cucumber supports localized Gherkin keywords for many languages) was not verified against a live Thai-language Cucumber project. Treat "keep in English" as the pragmatic default for internal Thai teams unless the project's own BDD framework config says otherwise.

## Business rules documentation

- **[Project decision]** A business rule statement should be a single unambiguous conditional (if X then Y), not prose that mixes rationale and rule in one sentence. Rationale (why the rule exists) belongs in a separate note, not folded into the rule text — this keeps the rule itself grep-able and stable even if the justification changes later.
- **[Open question]** No single authoritative external source was found specifically for "how to phrase a business rule as opposed to a requirement" — this is closer to established BA/requirements-engineering practice (e.g., IIBA BABOK covers business rules as a distinct artifact type from requirements) than to a single style guide. Flagged as needing a deeper pass if the skill later needs to strongly prescribe business-rule phrasing.

## Internal engineering / stakeholder communication

- **[Project decision]** Internal engineering communication (Slack threads, PR descriptions, internal Confluence/README-style docs) should default to the register of a competent colleague talking to another colleague: direct, unpadded, no bureaucratic throat-clearing (ดำเนินการ, ทั้งนี้, ดังนั้น used as connective tissue rather than because the register calls for it). Stakeholder-facing summaries (status updates to non-engineering stakeholders, executive summaries) can legitimately sit more formal, because the audience and the stakes of misreading are different — this is an audience-driven tone shift, not a blanket "be more formal for business writing" rule. See [terminology-research.md](terminology-research.md) and the audience model in the main skill for how this should be operationalized.
- **[Observed pattern]** AI-generated Thai business writing tends to default to a single high-formality register regardless of whether the audience is an internal dev team or an external executive, which is the actual failure mode this skill needs to catch — not "business Thai should be less formal" as a blanket rule, but "business Thai formality should track audience, and AI output currently doesn't vary it."

## Audience-based register research

Tagging note: this section introduces a 5th tag, **[HYPOTHESIS]** — a reasoned claim with no source behind it, weaker than `[Observed pattern]` (which at least reflects something seen, not just inferred). Existing tags elsewhere in this file are unchanged.

- **[Source-backed principle]** Audience-adaptation is an established, general (non-Thai-specific) communication principle: tailor content, tone, and delivery to the reader's organizational role (peer/supervisor/subordinate), existing level of knowledge, and likely reaction — not to a fixed formality default. A commonly cited framing: formal language suits regulators/stakeholders, casual language suits customers in relationship-driven contexts — but this is a starting heuristic, not a rule, since "customer" also covers regulated-industry correspondence that stays formal (see `contexts/customer-facing.md` in the skill). (Lumen Learning, "Audience," *Communication for Professionals*; Fleximize, "How businesses should adapt communication depending on the audience")
- **[Source-backed principle, Thai-specific]** Thailand is documented in intercultural-communication research as a high-context culture where meaning is often conveyed indirectly, and a direct "no" is uncommon — softer alternatives ("ยาก", "ลองดูก่อน", silence) are used instead. This is a real, academically-discussed feature of Thai communication norms, not a stereotype invented for this project. (Multiple sources converge: academic paper on Thai-Korean intercultural business communication problems; general Thailand cross-cultural business guides)
- **[Source-backed principle, Thai-specific, contested framing]** "เกรงใจ" (kreng jai) — regard for others' feelings, avoiding imposition or confrontation — is repeatedly identified as a defining feature of Thai workplace culture, and is linked in the literature to reluctance to disagree with superiors or deliver direct negative feedback. **Caveat**: much of the readily available material on this (cross-cultural business-advice blogs, LinkedIn posts) is closer to popular cultural commentary than rigorous empirical research, similar in kind to how Hofstede's cultural-dimensions framework is widely cited but also widely critiqued as reductive. Treat "kreng jai shapes Thai business writing" as a plausible, repeatedly-observed claim worth taking seriously — not as a settled, quantified finding.
- **[Project decision]** What this implies for the skill: kreng jai/high-context norms are a reason internal Thai business writing may already lean indirect by cultural default — which cuts *against* this skill's stance in `core/tone.md` that professional ≠ automatically formal, in one specific way: an internal message that seems "too blunt" to a Thai reader might not be a formality problem so much as a directness problem. The skill's existing guidance (state the rule/fact directly, keep rationale separate — see `requirements.md`, this file's Business rules section) is aimed at *requirements/rules*, where directness is a correctness requirement regardless of cultural register norms. It should not be read as "always maximize bluntness in all internal Thai business writing" — bad-news delivery, disagreement, and feedback to a superior are contexts where some cultural softening is expected and appropriate, distinct from a testable rule/requirement statement. `[OPEN QUESTION]`: exactly where the line sits between "this needs to stay direct because it's a rule/spec" and "this can stay indirect because it's interpersonal" needs native-speaker judgment, not a rule this research can settle from outside.
- **[HYPOTHESIS]** No Thai-language analogue to the English-speaking "plain language movement" (the government/legal writing-reform tradition behind, e.g., the US Plain Writing Act) was found in this search pass. This doesn't mean one doesn't exist — it means a dedicated search this pass didn't surface one. Absent that, this skill's plain-language stance for Thai (see `core/clarity.md`, `contexts/documentation.md`) is a `[PROJECT DECISION]` built by analogy to the English-language movement's principles, not backed by an equivalent Thai institutional reform effort. `[OPEN QUESTION]`: worth a dedicated follow-up search using different Thai search terms, or asking whether any Thai government agency (e.g. ก.พ., สำนักงานพัฒนารัฐบาลดิจิทัล) has published plain-language guidance for public-facing digital services specifically — digital government services are exactly the kind of place this might exist even if general "government writing reform" doesn't surface it.

## What this explicitly excludes

Per scope: FAB/AIDA/PAS frameworks, ad-claims/legal-compliance checklists (อย./สคบ.), and platform-specific commercial copy (Shopee/Lazada titles, social captions) are not part of this skill and were not researched here — they belong to advertising copywriting, a separate domain.
